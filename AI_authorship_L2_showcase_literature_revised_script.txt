Slide 1 — Title
Good morning everyone. Today I will present my research proposal titled “Psycholinguistic Cues in AI-Authorship Perception and L2 Text Memory.” This presentation follows the third option of the showcase: an original research idea based on the course content. My project focuses on how Chinese-English bilingual students judge whether an English text is human-written, AI-generated, or human-AI hybrid. It also asks whether these text types affect memory during second-language reading. The main idea is that people may not directly detect AI authorship. Instead, they may rely on linguistic cues such as fluency, structure, emotional expression, and personal voice.

Slide 2 — Presentation Route
To make the route clear, I am choosing Option 3: an original research proposal. I will first explain where the idea comes from, then summarize the key literature that motivates the research gap, then introduce the variables and three experimental tasks, and finally show how the study could be implemented through a web demo. I am not doing a critical comparison of two articles. The literature is used to support the research gap and motivate my own design.

Slide 3 — Why This Topic Matters
AI-generated writing is becoming common in school and daily life. People often say a text “sounds like AI,” but that judgment may not be based on actual authorship. A text may sound AI-generated because it is very fluent, highly organized, emotionally neutral, or generic. However, these features can also appear in human writing, especially after editing. So my research is not simply about AI detection. It is about a psycholinguistic question: how do readers use language features to infer who wrote a text? This connects to language perception, reading comprehension, pragmatics, second-language processing, and working memory.

Slide 4 — Literature Foundation
I looked at four pieces of literature to support this project. Chein and colleagues in 2024 studied human ability to distinguish AI-generated from human content and found that people can perform above chance, but individual differences are large. Weber-Wulff and colleagues in 2023 tested AI detection tools and argued that they are not consistently reliable, especially for edited or obfuscated texts. Elkhatat and colleagues also showed that AI detector performance changes across models and can produce false positives. Finally, Goldstein and colleagues in 2025 discussed large language models as computational models relevant to human language processing. Together, these studies suggest a gap: we know that both humans and tools struggle with AI-text identification, but we know less about what linguistic cues readers use, and whether AI versus human texts affect second-language memory encoding.

Slide 5 — Research Contribution
My contribution is to move from an accuracy problem to a mechanism problem. Existing studies often ask whether people or tools can correctly distinguish AI writing. My study asks what linguistic cues guide that judgment. Existing detection studies focus on correct or incorrect classification. My study focuses on psycholinguistic processes: perceived cues, authorship judgment, and L2 memory encoding. The originality is that it links AI-authorship perception with second-language memory tasks.

Slide 6 — Research Questions and Variables
My study has two research questions. First, what linguistic cues make an English text sound AI-generated? Second, do AI-generated and human-written texts differ in L2 memory encoding? The main independent variable is text type: human-written, AI-generated, and human-AI hybrid. The dependent variables include authorship judgment accuracy, AI-likelihood rating, cue ratings such as fluency, structure, emotion, and personal voice, and L2 memory accuracy and reaction time. The control variables include English-only texts, a text length of around eighty to one hundred and twenty words, matched topic and vocabulary level, and participants’ bilingual background and English proficiency.

Slide 7 — Materials and Stimulus Control
The materials will be short English passages. I will use English because the participants are Chinese-English bilingual students, so English functions as the second language in this study. To control the materials, I will prepare matched text sets. A human-written paragraph will be paired with an AI-generated version on the same topic, with similar length and vocabulary level. For human-AI hybrid texts, I will use a standard editing rule, such as AI polishing a human text without changing the main content. This is important because the results should reflect differences in linguistic style, not differences in topic, length, or difficulty.

Slide 8 — Experiment 1
Experiment 1 is the AI-authorship judgment task. Participants read each English passage and answer: Who do you think wrote this text? The choices are human-written, AI-generated, human with AI editing, or not sure. This task measures intuitive authorship perception. The key outcomes are judgment accuracy, misclassification rate, and the proportion of each text type judged as AI-generated. For example, if many human-AI hybrid texts are judged as AI-generated, this may suggest that readers associate polished writing with AI authorship.

Slide 9 — Experiment 2
Experiment 2 is the linguistic cue rating task. After the authorship judgment, participants rate each passage on a one-to-five Likert scale. The dimensions include fluency, structural regularity, clarity, emotional expression, personal voice, genericness, and naturalness. This task breaks down the vague feeling of “AI-like writing” into measurable psycholinguistic cues. Later, I can examine which cue ratings are most strongly related to AI-likelihood judgments. For example, if texts with high structure and high genericness but low personal voice are more likely to be judged as AI-generated, these features may function as cues for AI-authorship perception.

Slide 10 — Experiment 3
Experiment 3 is the L2 text memory task. Participants read English passages and then complete a short distractor task, such as deciding whether a number is odd or even. This prevents them from simply repeating the text from short-term memory. Then they complete two memory tasks. The first is sentence recognition: they decide whether a sentence appeared in the passage. The second is word-order reconstruction: they see scrambled words from one sentence and try to reconstruct the correct sentence. The dependent variables are recognition accuracy, reconstruction accuracy, and reaction time. This moves the project beyond detection and into L2 reading, sentence processing, and working memory.

Slide 11 — Web Demo
At this point, I will briefly show the web demo for about sixty to ninety seconds. The demo includes a background survey, a reading page with a timer, the authorship judgment and cue rating questions, a short distractor task, and the L2 memory task. The purpose of the demo is to show that the proposal can be implemented as a real online experiment. It can collect judgment data, rating data, reading time, memory accuracy, and reaction time.

Slide 12 — Expected Results and Analysis
I expect three main patterns. First, texts that are highly fluent, structurally regular, generic, and emotionally neutral may be more likely to be judged as AI-generated. Second, AI-likeness may correlate positively with structure and genericness, and negatively with personal voice or emotional expression. Third, in the L2 memory task, AI texts may be processed smoothly because of regular structure, but they may not necessarily be remembered better. Human-written texts may sometimes support deeper memory encoding because they contain more distinctive or emotionally meaningful details. The basic analysis will compare AI-likelihood ratings by text type, correlate cue ratings with authorship judgments, and compare memory accuracy by text type.

Slide 13 — Conclusion and References
To conclude, this project meets the third showcase option because it is an original research proposal based on psycholinguistics. The literature motivates the gap, and the web demo shows how it could be implemented. The project reframes AI writing as a psycholinguistic question. Instead of simply asking whether people can detect AI writing, it asks what linguistic cues readers use to infer authorship and how different text types affect L2 memory. The final logic is: linguistic cues influence authorship perception, and text type may also influence L2 reading and memory performance. Thank you.
